Basic writing skills, often referred to as the mechanics of writing, include handwriting, spelling, sentence construc-
tion, and typing (Graham etal. 2012). The acquisition of 
these skills enables students to communicate their ideas in 
a variety of diﬀerent formats such as test responses, emails, 
and essays. Transcription skills, i.e., handwriting and spell-
ing compete for limited cognitive resources. If spelling and 
handwriting are taxing it can aﬀect the quality of student 
compositions. Vice versa, if basic skills such as handwrit-ing and spelling are automatic, then resources in working 
memory are freed for higher level processes such as plan-ning, generating language, and revising needed in written 
expression (Berninger etal. 1997). Although considered 
basic, these skills each have complexities and challenges 
of their own.Handwriting Handwriting is a perceptual-motor skill encompassing a blend of visual-motor coordination abili-ties, motor planning, cognitive, and perceptual skills, as well 
as tactile and kinesthetic sensitivities (Feder and Majnemer 
2007, p.313). Despite the increase of keyboarding, hand-
writing is a necessary life skill, essential for communica-
tion and everyday tasks. If handwriting is not automatic, the 
eﬀort required to produce a written text can place a strain on 
other writing processes (Graham etal. 2000), resulting in a 
negative impact on academic success (Feder and Majnemer 
2007).Elements of handwriting that contribute to overall leg-ibility include spacing between words and/or letters, as 
well as the formation (slant and size) of the letters (Gra-
ham etal. 2006). Handwriting fluency (legibility and speed) increases as students get older, although the rate of 
improvement slows. By the time students have completed 
elementary school, the improvement in handwriting has 
plateaued (Graham etal. 1998). Boys are more likely to 
exhibit handwriting ﬂuency problems than girls (Berninger 
etal. 1997; Graham etal. 1998), concerning since the ratio of boys to girls with ASD is approximately 5:1 (Centers 
for Disease Control and Prevention 2014).The writing task and quantity of writing required can impact handwriting legibility (Feder and Majnemer 2007; 
Maldarelli etal. 2015). In a study conducted by Graham etal. (2006), participants were asked to complete three dif-
ferent tasks, each one placing increased cognitive demands 
on the writer: (a) writing the alphabet from memory, 
(b) copying a paragraph, and (c) composition. On aver-age participants exhibited better spacing and alignment 
when copying versus composing. Those categorized as 
poor handwriters were more likely to produce letters that 
included extra strokes and to exhibit greater variability in 
letter placement. Instruction in handwriting is a factor in 
the quality of handwriting (Berninger etal. 1997). Without 
instruction, writers may develop habits that are detrimen-
tal to the overall quality of their written expression. Feder 
and Majnemer (2007) posit that motor planning is the best 
predictor of legibility, but suggest sensory awareness of 
ﬁngers may have an impact on writing performance which 
may be a concern for individuals with ASD. If the physical 
act of handwriting is too demanding, students may fail to 
develop a self-regulatory approach to writing content or 
they may avoid writing altogether (Graham etal. 2008; 
Graham and Harris 2006).Spelling Spelling is a transcription skill which involves placing letters in order to represent a word, so as to con-
vey meaning. Spell-check functions on electronic devices 
can assist writers with correct spelling, composition, and 
proofreading (Berninger 1999). According to triple word 
form theory, three kinds of processing are needed for cor-rect spelling: (a) phonological, (b) orthographic, and (c) 
morphological (Berninger etal. 2010). In the natural course 
of development, there is considerable variation in the rate 
children develop these processes, but patterns are evident. 
In the early stages, when children ﬁrst learn to write their 
name, they appear to replicate a visual pattern or create a 
logographic representation of the word (Berninger 1999; McNeill etal. 2013). Over time, students recognize posi-
tional constraints of certain letter combinations, e.g., ck, and learn to spell new words through analogy (Bourassa 
and Treiman 2001). As students get older, phonemic and 
phonological awareness become predictors of spelling abil-
ity (Garcia etal. 2010). Omission of letters in blends, or 
phonologically plausible spelling errors are common. As 
students recognize letter units and rimes, orthographic

which can result in students being less likely to choose 
words they cannot spell (Graham and Santangelo 2014). 
Transcription skills compete with text generation for cog-
nitive resources, so if transcription skills like handwriting 
and spelling are not automatized it can negatively impact 
other cognitive processes needed in written expression. It 
has been noted that individuals with ASD demonstrate a 
relative strength in rote learning, which may support spell-
ing (Mayes and Calhoun 2006). It is not clear whether IQ 
scores, or co-morbidity with other disorders contribute to 
low spelling scores found in some individuals with ASD.Sentence Construction
Syntax refers to how words are sequenced to form sentences. A sentence is often referred to as a complete thought, but 
there is more to sentence construction than putting thoughts 
into words. Translating thoughts into text requires careful 
selection of vocabulary, attention to syntax, and an under-standing of the reader (Myhill 2009). In order for a writer to 
construct sentences, he or she must have syntactical knowl-
edge which includes rules about word order, grammar, punc-
tuation, and function (Saddler 2013).Very young children entering school may write incom-plete sentences or add unneeded words, possibly because 
they write the way they talk, or do not recognize when they 
have completed a sentence. As early as ﬁrst grade, young 
children can produce both simple and complex clausal con-
structions in their writing (Berninger etal. 2010, p.153). 
When required most ﬁrst graders can produce a simple 
grammatically correct sentence.Although development is not homogenous, as students get older the length of their sentences increase in writing, 
but do not necessarily increase in speech (ODonnell 1967). 
In addition, students gain more syntactical control over their 
writing (ODonnell 1967; Berninger etal. 2010), and make 
greater use of passive verbs (Myhill 2009). Older students 
are able to create increasingly more complex sentences for a 
variety of purposes, such as narrative, descriptive, or persua-sive writing (Beers and Nagy 2011; Myhill 2009). Over time, grammar improves, and students are able to add details and 
include subordinate clauses. Older students are less likely to produce sentence fragments or run-on sentences. Evidence 
also suggests that girls write longer sentences than boys 
(ODonnell 1967). Studies analyzing sentence construction of individuals with ASD show mixed results. Some studies 
reveal that the syntactical abilities of individuals with ASD 
are relatively intact and are similar in length and complexity 
to their TD peers (Norbury and Bishop 2003). Whereas other 
studies show sentences written by individuals with ASD lack 
complexity (Myles etal. 2003). Some individuals with ASD 
show errors in referencing, e.g., pronouns do not agree with 
subjects and objects. This results in ambiguity (Norbury and 
Bishop 2003). It is not clear if this type of error is linked to social-communication deﬁcits in individuals with ASD or 
to the age of the individuals.Content
The content of written expression can be examined from two perspectives, the product and the process (Berninger etal. 
1996). Production of text follows a developmental pattern. 
Young children ﬁrst produce pictures without text, then rep-
resentations of letters, true letters, sentences and coherent 
pieces of writing. Students can distinguish between diﬀerent 
types of text (Beers and Nagy 2011), which include narra-
tive, descriptive, informative, and persuasive forms of writ-
ing at a young age. Within each type there are conventions 
related to organization and language choice, e.g., a persua-
sive letter written to a legislator would look very diﬀerent 
from a ﬂyer on persuading readers not to take up smoking.Developmentally, children progress from writing unre-lated sentences, to listing or chaining associated ideas, to 
creating a coherent, organized text. This organization is evi-
dent in paragraphs in which details are organized around a 
local topic, and in longer pieces in which local topics are 
organized around a global topic (Berninger etal. 1996). 
Desirable features of an organized narrative include setting, 
problem, sequenced events, and climax, whereas a hierar-chical structure developed around topic sentences is pre-
ferred in expository texts. Less skilled writers tend to use a 
knowledge-telling process in which relevant content is listed, 
whereas more mature writers transform this information in 
coherent, organized text.The process of writing refers to the functions of planning, organization, and revising. It appears that executive func-tions contribute to the writing process. Executive control is 
a term used to describe functions related to planning, organi-
zation, and cognitive ﬂexibility that enable an individual 
to obtain a particular goal. In writing, executive functions 
may govern processes in several ways: the retrieval of lin-
guistic information for encoding, the generation of ideas, 
planning, and the monitoring of behaviors needed to produce 
text (Altemeier etal. 2008). Atypical executive function is 
often evident in individuals with ASD (Kenworthy etal.

related to an individuals IQ, age and the complexity of the 
tasks presented. Context may also play a role in executive 
functioning, as individuals with ASD tend to perform bet-
ter in highly-structured settings (Kenworthy etal. 2008). 
Notably, working memory deﬁcits can impact the ability of 
individuals with ASD to retell a story, a task which requires 
the retrieval of relevant vocabulary, syntactical structure, 
and sequencing of events (Gabig 2008).Oral Language andEarly Literacy ExperiencesIt is also important to consider possible correlations between 
oral language skills and early literacy experiences on the 
writing performance of individuals with ASD. For individu-
als with speciﬁc language impairment (SLI), oral language 
measures on vocabulary and spelling have been found to be a 
signiﬁcant predictor of compositional quality (Dockrell and 
Connelly 2015). Processes associated with spelling (pho-nological, orthographical, morphological), learned through 
oral language may be affected in individuals with SLI. 
However, Browder etal. (2009) found that the correlation between phonemic awareness skills and becoming a success-
ful reader is not consistent in learners who display deﬁcits in 
oral language ability. Instead, impairments in oral language 
processing may impact the development of word meaning 
for learners with ASD (McIntyre etal. 2017), which in turn 
may impact text generation.Language experiences may account for some of the vari-ations found in the performance of individuals with ASD. 
Although alphabet knowledge is equivalent to preschool 
peers (Westerveld etal. 2016), parents reported that their 
children with ASD did not appear to understand why people 
read and write (Lanter etal. 2012). Dynia, Lawton, Logan, 
and Justice (2014) found diﬀerences in the home literacy 
environment of children with ASD, with less frequent oppor-tunities to participate in shared reading possibly explained 
by children not reciprocating an interest in literacy related 
opportunities.In a study comparing oral language problems in individu-als with language impairments and ASD, Dockrell, Rick-etts, Charman, and Lindsay (2014) found many participants 
with ASD were not able to complete a writing task, possibly 
because they did not understand the task demands or were 
unable to generate ideas. Participants who did not produce 
text were signiﬁcantly impaired on the Social Responsive-
ness Scale, a measure of the severity of social-communica-
tion impairments in autism. Evidence suggests that many 
individuals with ASD lack theory of mind (Baron-Cohen 
etal. 1985), meaning that they have diﬃculty interpret-ing the mental states of others. It has been suggested that 
a lack of theory of mind may contribute to impairments in understanding human relationships (Brown and Klein 
2011). Writing tasks often require the writer to understand 
the perspective of others, for example to recognize the view 
of the reader, the task demands, or multiple viewpoints. It is 
not known how the deﬁning social-communication impair-ments of individuals with ASD might inﬂuence writing 
performance.Need forStudyCurrent research has provided educators with a clear under-standing of patterns of reading ability in individuals with 
ASD (Brown etal. 2013; Huemer and Mann 2010; Nation 
etal. 2006), however less is known about the written expres-sion of individuals with ASD. A limited number of litera-
ture reviews related to individuals with ASD and writing 
were identiﬁed (Kushki etal. 2011; Pennington and Delano 
2012; Westerveld etal. 2016). No reviews were identiﬁed 
considering the overall writing ability of individuals with 
ASD compared to their typically developing peers. Kushki 
etal. (2011) conducted a review of handwriting diﬃcul-
ties and students with ASD. Pennington and Delano (2012) 
conducted a review of instructional strategies for teaching 
writing to students with ASD, and Westerveld etal. (2016) 
conducted a review of the emergent literacy skills of pre-
schoolers with ASD. Research synthesis is a systematic 
approach to present the current state of knowledge, and to 
highlight important issues that research has left uncovered 
(Cooper 2016). As such, the present study emerges as the ﬁrst meta-analysis of the writing ability of individuals with 
ASD. Wang etal. (2013) note that it is common to select 
one of two approaches, meta-analysis or quality examination 
checks, when conducting a synthesis of the research. The 
present synthesis utilizes both approaches. A meta-analysis 
was conducted using quantitative procedures and statisti-
cal analysis to synthesize the prior research. Additionally, 
quality indicators were applied to studies meeting inclusion 
criteria as part of the analysis. The purpose of this study was 
through the examination of existing research, to compare the 
written expression of individuals with ASD and their typi-
cally developing (TD) peers.The research questions under consideration follow: Are there diﬀerences between individuals with ASD and TD 
individuals in written expression? If so, in what compo-nents are there diﬀerences? Since many individuals with 
ASD exhibit grapho-motor deﬁcits, it was hypothesized that 
they would have signiﬁcantly lower scores in measures of 
handwriting. Due to cognitive diﬀerences aﬀecting execu-
tive function and social understanding established in prior 
research, it was hypothesized that diﬀerences in the writ-
ing performance of individuals with ASD would emerge in 
components related to sustained and connected language use 
and ﬂuency (e.g., content, length), and not in areas that are

search of studies (Cooper 2016). An electronic search of four 
databases, ERIC, Medline, PsychINFO, and SAGE yielded 
70 articles. Databases were searched using all combina-
tions of the following keywords, autism spectrum disorders, 
autism, writing, writing skills and writing development, to 
locate studies published between January 1985 and Decem-
ber 2015. Using the same search terms on Google Scholar 
yielded an additional eight articles. Issues of the ﬁve most 
recent years of the following journals were hand-searched 
for relevant articles: Autism, Focus on Autism and Develop-
mental Disabilities, Journal of Autism and Developmental 
Disorders. These journals were selected for their impact 
ranking, except in the case of Focus on Autism and Devel-
opmental Disabilities, which was selected for its educational 
focus. Next, references from studies meeting inclusion crite-
ria were reviewed to identify any additional studies meeting 
the inclusionary criteria. Finally, references from previous 
reviews were checked for relevant articles (Kushki etal. 
2011; Westerveld etal. 2016). The researchers examined 
abstracts and methodologies from 82 studies, and identiﬁed 
20% of the articles (n = 16) as appropriate for full review. Abstracts and methods were reviewed against the inclusion 
and exclusion criteria by both authors independently. Using 
the point-by-point approach (Kazdin 1982) inter-rater agree-
ment was calculated at 87.5%. For the remaining studies, 
agreement was reached on whether or not the study should 
be included in the review. Two studies (Goldman 2008; Losh 
and Capps 2003) assessed transcribed oral records of par-ticipants stories, which did not meet the inclusion criteria. 
One study (Mayes and Calhoun 2007) could not be included 
in this meta-analysis as relevant data were not obtainable.A 
total of 13 studies were included within the meta-analysis.Inclusion andExclusion CriteriaStudies were selected for this meta-analysis using the fol-
lowing predetermined criteria: (a) the study was published 
in English in a peer-reviewed journal between 2000 and 
2015; (b) the participants in the study were individuals with 
ASD were diagnosed with either autism, ASD, Aspergers 
syndrome, pervasive developmental disorder, or pervasive 
developmental disorder-not otherwise specified; (c) the 
study included written expression as a dependent variable. 
Measures of written expression were derived from samples in which participants produced connected meaningful text, 
such as spelling, sentences, or paragraphs. Text could be 
either handwritten or typed; (d) the study had a research 
design which included at least two comparison groups, one 
comprised of individuals with ASD and one comprised of 
TD individuals; (e) data on each group could be parsed out 
for analysis, i.e., means, standard deviations, and sample 
sizes could be obtained. If mean scores were not reported 
in the article, the corresponding author was contacted to 
request means and standard deviations. Exclusionary criteria 
were also applied. Studies in which participants with ASD 
could not be parsed out from the sample were excluded. 
Studies which included text dictated to a scribe were also 
excluded.Coding ProceduresAttribute coding was used primarily for obtaining informa-
tion from the studies, and the frequencies of each attribute 
were calculated for a descriptive summary. Attribute coding 
enabled the researchers to categorize the studies according 
to their dependent variables. Each study was coded for ﬁve 
descriptive attributes: (a) focus of journal, (b) number of 
participants, (c) age of participants, (d) inclusion criteria, 
and, (e) dependent variables.Quality indicators were developed to assess the quality of the studies. A coding system based on quality indicators was 
developed by the researchers using procedures described by 
Cooper (2016). Although no interventions were adminis-

indicators were based on the recommendations of Gersten 
etal. (2005). The 15 quality indicators (deﬁned in Table1) broadly ﬁt into three categories: (a) ﬁdelity in procedures for

 Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders (2018) 48:868–882Eﬀect SizesEﬀect sizes ranged from 0.28 to 0.75 across the compo-nents of size (0.75), length (0.67), legibility (0.66), speed 
(0.65), letter formation (0.62), structure (0.55), sentence 
construction (0.29) and spelling (0.28). Correlations were 
conducted to investigate the relationship of study quality 
and the eﬀect sizes of the six writing components identiﬁed 
with signiﬁcant diﬀerences between individuals with ASD 
and TD individuals (size, length, legibility, speed, structure, 
and spelling). There was little relation found between the 
quality indicator scores and the magnitude of study eﬀect 
sizes. Of note, while not signiﬁcant, negative correlations 
were found between eﬀect size and study quality in four of 
the six components: size, length, legibility, and structure.DiscussionIt was hypothesized that the writing performance of indi-
viduals with ASD would be signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from their 
typically developing peers in measures of handwriting, and 
in the components that required sustained and connected lan-
guage use, e.g., length, and content. Conversely, signiﬁcant 
diﬀerences would not be found in spelling or sentence con-
struction. This study compared the performance in written 
expression of individuals with ASD and their TD counter-parts by conducting a meta-analysis using data from previ-
ously published studies. All thirteen studies selected were 
used in the meta-analysis. There were signiﬁcant diﬀerences 
in performance in six of the eight components of writing 
analyzed; (a) length, (b) legibility, (c) size, (d) speed, (e) 
spelling, and (f) structure. Results conﬁrmed the hypothesis 
that individuals with ASD would have lower scores in hand-
writing. The handwriting of ASD participants was bigger in 
size and scored lower on measures of legibility. Contrary 
to the hypothesis, spelling scores were signiﬁcantly lower 
for participants with ASD. Apart from letter formation, 
individuals with ASD scored lower in measures related to 
transcription skills (handwriting and spelling). This could be 
due to grapho-motor diﬀerences in individuals with ASD. 
However, results may be skewed due to the high proportion 
of boys with ASD, thereby reﬂecting established patterns 
of handwriting problems in boys (Berninger etal. 1997; 
Graham etal. 1998). Although phonological awareness is a predictor of spelling ability (Garcia etal. 2010), other explanations for lower spelling scores in individuals with 
ASD must be considered. Other elements of oral language 
e.g., vocabulary development (McIntyre etal. 2017) and morphological processes, need to be examined further in 
individuals with ASD to secure an understanding of their 
spelling development.Given the relationship between transcription skills and content (Berninger etal. 1997), ﬁndings on the components 
of length and content were unsurprising. Participants with 
ASD wrote less and at a slower rate than TD participants. 
On rubric indicators measuring the structure of writing, par-ticipants with ASD scored signiﬁcantly lower. The ﬁndings 
of this study suggest individuals with ASD experience dif-
ﬁculties with written expression, and those diﬃculties are 
analogous to those experienced by other individuals identi-
ﬁed by researchers as poor writers (Berninger 1999; Graham 
etal. 2008; Graham and Harris 2006). As hypothesized there were no signiﬁcant diﬀerences found in the component of 
sentence construction, which suggests that like their typi-
cally developing peers, individuals with ASD are beneﬁtting 
from existing educational interventions in this area.There were no signiﬁcant correlations between quality indicator scores and the eﬀect sizes (Hedges g) of measures included in the meta-analysis on those variables that were 
found to be signiﬁcant. All the studies included in the pre-
sent study were awarded a quality indicator score of 8 or 
above. It appears that the quality of the all the studies were 
suﬃcient and it is unlikely that variations in quality indica-
tor scores aﬀected the overall ﬁndings of the meta-analysis.LimitationsThe ﬁndings of this meta-analysis should be viewed with 
caution, as there are several limitations to the study. First, the 
sample size is small. Only 13 studies met criteria for inclu-
sion, and although these studies yielded a total of 98 diﬀer-ent measurements, only 35 were used for meta-analysis. A 
small sample size can also impact eﬀect sizes which were 
small to medium, yet a larger sample might not impact eﬀect 
sizes. In nine studies, participants were matched for either 
gender, age or IQ to prevent sampling errors. In this context, 
the small to medium eﬀect sizes suggest, despite diﬀerence 
in underlying etiology, writing performance in this subset of 
individuals is not too dissimilar to other students with learn-
ing diﬃculties, and their writing development may follow a 
similar trajectory to their typically developing peers.A second issue was the range of measures. Studies reported data on diﬀerent measures of the same concept, 
e.g., length, or included measures created from other meas-
ures e.g., percentages, thus making it diﬃcult to ﬁnd com-
parable measures. Insuﬃcient data were available to analyze 
measures in vocabulary and pragmatics, thus resulting in a 
limited view of written expression by individuals with ASD. 
A third limitation is the variability of participants. Nearly 
all the studies included participants with a veriﬁable clinical 
diagnosis of ASD. IQ scores greater than 70 were listed in 
the inclusion criteria of seven studies, as such results may 
be skewed. Strict criteria regarding IQ and oral language 
abilities may result in samples not reﬂecting the variability 
879Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders (2018) 48:868–882 in IQ (Griswold etal. 2002), vocabulary (Barnhill etal. 2000), and oral language (Westerveld etal. 2016) found in 
the overall population of individuals with ASD. Since the 
incidence of ASD is approximately 1 in 68 (CDC 2014), 
random selection of participants from a larger group could 
be considered in future studies. Across studies the ages of 
participants varied considerably. Measures were sometimes 
related to the age of participant e.g., in Lanter etal. (2013) 
emerging literacy skills were measured, whereas in Brown 
and Kleins analysis (2011), adult participants wrote a com-
plete piece of persuasive prose. A fourth limitation is that 
the studies grouped by writing component did not always 
use the same assessment. Measures were not always identi-
cal, e.g., in the measurement of speed Fuentes etal., (2009) 
measured rate of speed over 2.5.min, whereas Hellinckx 
etal. (2013) measured rate of speed over 5min. It is not 
clear if the length of time spent writing impacted partici-
pants performance in speed or other variables. Likewise it 
is impossible to determine how the lack of correspondence 
in measures impacted overall results.Fifth, in considering a relationship between the magni-tude of eﬀect size and quality of studies resulting in a sig-
niﬁcant diﬀerence between individuals with ASD and TD 
individuals, the range of eﬀect sizes was limited in variabil-
ity with ﬁve of the six falling between 0.6 and 0.7, poten-
tially limiting the signiﬁcance of correlations with quality 
indicators. Finally, a low quality indicator score could be 
considered a limitation. Many quality indicators pertain to 
ideal sampling methods (e.g., random sampling and power 
analysis to determine sample size) which are diﬃcult to 
attain with a population of individuals with ASD. Lack of 
data on gender or IQ prohibited analysis of subgroups.Implications forResearchThe results of this meta-analysis suggest that individuals 
with ASD struggle with written expression. In many respects 
individuals with ASD exhibit traits similar to other indi-
viduals with learning problems. It appears that diﬃculties 
in handwriting and spelling aﬀect the overall quality of writ-
ten composition. However, many questions remain. It is not 
clear whether individuals with ASD exhibit diﬀerences in 
vocabulary use or in the use of pragmatic language, such as 
that needed for character development. Further research also 
needs to be done to determine if individuals with ASD have 
strength in writing for a particular purpose. For example, 
do they perform better in expository writing tasks than in 
persuasive writing tasks?Many of the studies included in this meta-analysis used standardized measures. However, few standardized 
measures exist for assessing diﬀerent genres of writing. 
As these measures are developed, researchers should seek to establish reliability and validity so that comparisons 
between and within populations can be reasonably made.Although sampling methods may be limited by geo-graphical area and availability when studying a population 
with ASD, this should not deter researchers from address-
ing other quality indicators. This meta-analysis did not 
calculate eﬀect sizes for subgroups such as gender and 
age. As more data are collected, it is important researchers 
report data for these subgroups. This is necessary to deter-mine, for example, how much of the variation in handwrit-
ing between individuals with ASD and typically develop-
ing individuals can be attributed to gender or to diagnosis.Finally, it is also important that researchers consider the needs of individuals with ASD whose IQ scores are 
below 70. Does their development of written expression 
correspond with individuals with intellectual disabilities, 
or does it exhibit unique traits? Identifying speciﬁc deﬁ-
cits of individuals with ASD will enable researchers and 
educators to develop strategies to better support their aca-
demic needs.Implications forPracticeThis study found that individuals with ASD have diﬃcul-
ties with handwriting, spelling and the structure of writing. 
Patterns in performance are not dissimilar to other individu-
als who struggle with written expression. Current research 
is focused on whether existing strategies to improve writ-
ten expression in students with learning diﬃculties will be 
eﬀective for individuals with ASD (Pennington and Delano 
2012). If the needs of individuals with ASD are similar to 
other individuals with learning problems, then educators can 
feel more conﬁdent with placing students with ASD in more 
inclusive environments to receive those same interventions.Students with ASD are a heterogeneous group, as such educators need to recognize that not all their students learn-
ing needs will be the same. Educators are advised to evaluate 
writing samples for length, legibility, size, speed, spelling, 
syntax, and structure using standardized measures, curricu-
lum-based measurements and/or rubrics. Once student needs 
are identiﬁed, educators will then be better able to target 
accommodations and interventions. Educators also need to 
be aware that inherent characteristics of ASD may impact 
components of written expression (e.g., vocabulary, perspec-
tive taking in persuasive writing) which existing research 
does not suﬃciently address. Educators should consider 
research-based practices designed for other populations to 
address learning needs in these areas. It is recommended the 
progress of each individual with ASD be closely monitored 
to determine the eﬀectiveness of interventions.Funding No funding was received in regard to this study.
